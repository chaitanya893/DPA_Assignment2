## 1. What is a Fund?
An **investment fund** is a pooled investment vehicle. It aggregates capital from multiple individual and institutional investors to purchase a diversified portfolio of securities, such as equities, fixed-income instruments, money-market assets, or other financial contracts.

* **Core Structure:** Rather than purchasing individual securities directly, investors acquire shares or units of the fund. The fund is managed according to a defined investment mandate or index methodology.
* **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The per-share asset value of a fund is represented by its Net Asset Value (NAV), generally calculated as total fund assets minus liabilities divided by the number of outstanding shares:
  $$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Outstanding Shares}}$$
* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  If a fund holds assets valued at \$10,000,000 and liabilities of \$500,000 with 1,000,000 shares outstanding, its NAV per share is \$9.50.

## 2. Mutual Fund
A **Mutual Fund** (commonly structured as an open-end investment company in the US or an open-end mutual fund trust/corporation in Canada) issues and redeems shares directly with investors.

* **Valuation & Trading:** Unlike exchange-traded securities, mutual fund shares are typically bought and sold at the forward-calculated NAV computed after the relevant market close.
* **Share Classes & Series:** A single mutual fund portfolio frequently offers multiple share classes or series (such as retail, advisor, or institutional series) designed for different fee structures or distribution channels. While each class shares the same underlying asset pool, each may have distinct expense ratios, NAVs, and per-share distribution rates.
* **Identifiers:** US mutual funds are referenced by standard market tickers, CUSIPs, and SEC EDGAR Series and Class IDs. Canadian mutual funds are commonly referenced by FundServ codes, CUSIPs, and SEDAR identifiers.
* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  An investor places an order to purchase shares of a mutual fund during the business day. The order settles based on the net asset value calculated following market close.

---

## 3. ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)
An **Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF)** is an open-end fund or unit investment trust whose shares trade on public stock exchanges (such as NYSE Arca, Nasdaq, or the Toronto Stock Exchange - TSX) throughout the standard trading session.

* **Intraday Trading:** ETF units are bought and sold at prevailing market prices that fluctuate throughout the trading day based on supply, demand, and underlying asset movements.
* **Creation and Redemption:** Institutional market participants (Authorized Participants) interact with the ETF issuer to create or redeem large share blocks, which helps market trading prices track closely to the fund's underlying value.
* **Identifiers:** ETFs in the US and Canada are identified by exchange ticker symbols, CUSIPs, ISINs, and regulatory filings.
* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  An investor purchases 50 units of an ETF through an exchange at 2:00 PM at the current market bid/ask price.

---

## 4. What is a Distribution?
A **distribution** is a payment made by a mutual fund or ETF to its unitholders or shareholders, passing through income or realized capital gains generated by the underlying portfolio.

* **Pass-Through Principle:** Under applicable tax frameworks in the US (Internal Revenue Code) and Canada (Income Tax Act), investment funds generally distribute net investment income and net realized capital gains to unitholders to maintain pass-through tax status.
* **Economic Effect on NAV:** A distribution represents a transfer of value from the fund's asset base to unitholders. As a general economic effect, on the ex-dividend date, the fund's NAV typically adjusts downward by the per-share distribution amount, subject to normal market fluctuations.
* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  If a fund has an NAV of \$50.00 and distributes \$1.50 per share, the fund's NAV generally adjusts downward by approximately \$1.50 on the ex-dividend date, reflecting the distribution payment.

---

## 5. Dividend Distribution (Net Investment Income)
A **dividend distribution** represents the pass-through of income earned by the fund's underlying securities:
* Dividends received from underlying equity holdings.
* Interest earned on debt securities, bonds, or money-market instruments.

After subtracting fund operating expenses and management fees from gross income, the remaining net investment income is distributed to shareholders on a regular schedule (such as monthly, quarterly, semi-annually, or annually).

* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  An income fund collects interest and dividends from its bond and equity holdings. After deducting expenses, it distributes \$0.25 per share as an income dividend.

---

## 6. Capital Gain Distribution
A **capital gain distribution** occurs when a fund realizes net profits from selling underlying securities for more than their purchase price (cost basis).

* **Realized Gains:** Portfolio rebalancing, index changes, or managing unitholder redemptions can lead to security sales. When realized gains exceed realized losses over the fund's tax year, the net gain is distributed to unitholders.
* **Short-Term vs. Long-Term:** 
  * In the US tax system, gains from assets held for one year or less are classified as short-term capital gains, while gains from assets held longer than one year are long-term capital gains.
  * In Canada, capital gains distributions are subject to statutory capital gains inclusion rules.
* **Timing:** Capital gain distributions are frequently declared toward the end of the calendar year (such as November or December), although funds may declare them at other times as operational needs dictate.
* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  At the end of its tax year, a fund calculates net realized gains from stock sales and declares a \$1.80 per share capital gain distribution.

## 7. Distribution Dates and Event Keying

A fund distribution involves several milestone dates that must be captured from authoritative source documents:

| Date Type | Description |
|:---|:---|
| **1. Declaration Date** (Announcement Date) | The date on which the fund company or board officially announces the distribution, per-share amounts, and schedule. |
| **2. Ex-Dividend Date (Ex-Date)** | The date that determines entitlement to the distribution. An investor buying on or after the ex-date does not receive the declared distribution. On this date, the fund's NAV generally adjusts for the payout. |
| **3. Record Date** | The date on which an investor must be listed on the fund's books to be eligible for the distribution. |
| **4. Payable Date (Payment Date)** | The date on which the cash distribution is credited or disbursed to investors. |
| **5. Reinvestment Date** | The date on which distributions are reinvested into additional shares for participating unitholders. |

### **Why Confusing These Dates Corrupts a Database:**
Confusing or conflating these dates directly corrupts downstream analytics, pricing models, and data pipelines:
* **Conflating Declaration Date with Ex-Date:** Distorts price-adjustment and corporate-action time series. The economic detachment of cash occurs on the ex-dividend date, not when the fund board announces it.
* **Keying on Payable Date:** Fund companies frequently shift payable dates due to administrative processing or banking holidays without altering the underlying economic distribution. Keying on payable date results in duplicate entries or fragmented historical series.
* **Mixing Record Date and Ex-Date:** Leads to shareholder entitlement calculation errors and settlement mismatches.
* **Conflating Reinvestment Date with Ex-Date:** Miscalculates portfolio cost basis and unit reinvestment timing for DRIP accounts.

### **Which Date Should the Event Be Keyed On, and Why?**
> **Rule:** Every distribution event must be uniquely keyed on:
> `(share_class_id, ex_dividend_date, distribution_category, estimated_or_final)`
> with a `version` number for restatements.

* **Economic Rationale:** The ex-dividend date is the exact point of economic separation where NAV adjusts for the declared distribution amount.
* **Why the category is in the key:** One share class can pay more than one distribution on the same ex-date. The typical case is December, when a fund pays its regular income dividend and a separate long-term capital gain distribution on the same day. Keyed on ex-date alone, the second payment would overwrite (or supersede) the first. The category (income, long-term gain, short-term gain, return of capital, special...) keeps them as two rows.
* **Why estimated/final is in the key:** An October estimate and the December final figure are two different facts and must both be kept (see section 10).
* **Processing Stability:** Unlike payable dates that may experience processing shifts, the ex-dividend date is economically definitive.

## 8. US Distribution Component Types
Under US regulatory requirements (including SEC disclosures and IRS Form 1099-DIV reporting), fund distributions are categorized into specific components:

1. **Ordinary Income Dividend:** Income derived from portfolio dividends, interest, and short-term capital gains.
2. **Qualified Dividend:** Dividends from qualifying corporations that meet statutory holding requirements, eligible for favorable tax rates.
3. **Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG):** Net realized gains from securities held for one year or less.
4. **Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG):** Net realized gains from securities held for more than one year.
5. **Return of Capital (ROC / Non-Dividend Distribution):** A distribution exceeding the fund's current and accumulated earnings. It generally reduces an investor's tax basis rather than being immediately taxable as income. SEC Rule 19a-1 requires written notices (Section 19(a) notices) when distributions include sources other than net investment income.
6. **Foreign Tax Paid / Credit:** Foreign withholding taxes paid by the fund on international holdings, passed through to investors.
7. **Section 199A Dividend:** Eligible distribution income derived from Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs).
8. **Tax-Exempt Income:** Interest income derived from qualifying municipal or governmental obligations.

* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  A US fund distribution of \$1.0000 per share may be composed of:
  * Ordinary Dividend: \$0.5000 (Qualified: \$0.3500, Non-Qualified: \$0.1500)
  * Return of Capital: \$0.2000
  * Long-Term Capital Gain: \$0.3000
  * **Component Sum:** \$0.5000 + \$0.2000 + \$0.3000 = \$1.0000 per share.

## 9. Canadian Distribution Component Types
In Canada, mutual fund trusts, corporations, and ETFs characterize distributions under Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) guidelines via CDS (Canadian Depository for Securities) and year-end tax slips (T3/T5):

1. **Eligible Dividend:** Dividends from Canadian corporations eligible for the enhanced Canadian dividend tax credit.
2. **Non-Eligible Dividend:** Dividends from Canadian corporations taxed at general small business rates.
3. **Capital Gains:** Net realized taxable capital gains from underlying investments.
4. **Return of Capital (ROC):** Distribution amounts in excess of net taxable income that reduce the investor's Adjusted Cost Base (ACB).
5. **Foreign Income (Foreign Non-Business Income):** Income earned from foreign investments.
6. **Foreign Tax Paid:** Foreign withholding taxes paid to foreign jurisdictions.
7. **Interest and Other Income:** Income earned from debt instruments, interest, and other sources.

* **Illustrative Concept:**
  *(Illustrative example — not real fund data)*
  A Canadian ETF distribution of \$0.40000 CAD per unit may be composed of:
  * Eligible Dividend: \$0.25000
  * Capital Gains: \$0.10000
  * Return of Capital: \$0.05000
  * **Component Sum:** \$0.25000 + \$0.10000 + \$0.05000 = \$0.40000 CAD per unit.

## 10. Estimated vs. Final Year-End Capital Gains

Fund sponsors typically communicate annual capital gains in two distinct phases:

1. **Estimated Phase (Autumn / Q4):** Fund families frequently publish preliminary distribution estimates (as projected ranges or estimates per share) to assist investors and advisors with year-end tax planning.
2. **Final Declaration Phase (Year-End):** The fund formally calculates realized gains up to the designated record date and issues the final, binding distribution amounts.

### **Data Management Principle: Coexistence**
* Preliminary estimates and final declarations represent distinct points in time.
* An estimate must not be silently overwritten or erased when a final figure is released.
* Both figures should coexist as distinct, versioned records with their respective source document links, preserving the full disclosure history.

---

## 11. Canadian T3/T5 Year-End Reallocations

Canadian mutual funds and ETFs often make periodic distributions during the year based on interim estimates. However, the final statutory tax breakdown across components is established after the fund's tax year-end.

* **Year-End CDS Factor Breakdowns:** In the early months of the following calendar year, fund managers release final CDS tax factor schedules (for T3/T5 tax reporting).
* **Restatement of Component Breakdown:** This annual reporting may reallocate the character of distributions paid earlier in the year (for example, adjusting the proportion between foreign income, capital gains, and return of capital).

### **Data Management Principle: Non-Destructive Versioning**
* Historical distribution records should not be destructively altered in place when year-end reallocations are published.
* Instead, updated tax component breakdowns are appended as new versions and linked to the prior record, maintaining a complete, auditable record of what was originally announced versus what was finalized.

## 12. Why These Concepts Matter for Assignment 2

A firm grounding in these financial domain concepts directly informs the architecture of the Detection and Extraction Engine:

1. **Two-Tier Processing Strategy:** Because distribution declarations occur intermittently throughout the year, an efficient detection layer can monitor for announcement activity before invoking deeper document extraction.
2. **Mathematical Data Quality Checks:** Knowing that individual distribution components must sum to the stated total distribution per share provides an objective, deterministic check on extracted numbers.
3. **Date Verification:** Understanding the purpose of declaration, ex-dividend, record, and payable dates enables the system to detect date anomalies or misaligned disclosures from source materials.
4. **Authoritative Source Awareness:** Recognizing specific filing types—such as SEC Rule 19a-1 notices for Return of Capital or Canadian CDS tax factor schedules—directs the system to the most reliable primary source documents.
5. **Idempotency and Versioning:** Recognizing that estimates evolve into final declarations and Canadian tax components undergo year-end restatement dictates an idempotent data design where historical data is preserved rather than overwritten.
